## Singapore sweetener and botanical rules

- Permitted sweeteners in water-based flavoured drinks (Food Regulations, Reg 18 and 13th Schedule): acesulfame-K up to 350 ppm, sucralose up to 300 ppm, steviol glycosides up to 160 ppm as steviol, neotame up to 20 ppm. Erythritol is permitted under good manufacturing practice. Monk fruit extract is permitted when it meets the Reg 18(3B) specification.
- A fruit-drink classification would reduce the steviol cap to 125 ppm and add a children advisory, so the product classification is a live question.
- Botanicals: SFA's record of views (9 September 2025) permits only whole extracts unless a specification says otherwise. Citrus fruit, citrus fibre, Citrus aurantium and calamansi are on the view list. No separate lemon or yuzu entry was found.
- Novel-food gate: a substance without at least 20 years of food use needs SFA approval before sale. Each named botanical needs this check (gate K6).
- Health and therapeutic claims are barred (Reg 9(2), amended January 2026). Wellness wording on a Singapore label is not available unless it falls under a Reg 9A permitted claim. None of the permitted claims covers botanicals.
- Sugar-free claim: 0.5 g or less per 100 ml, with an energy statement. No added sugar is also available when a sweetener is used.

## Korea white space

- Seven citrus zero or sugar-free sparkling SKUs from five brands are on sale in 2026. These include Singha's own Lemon, Yuzu and Lime Lemon zero range, a direct competitor that shows the category is occupied.
- Third-party citrus zero competitors: Lotte Chilsung Cider Zero Yuja (KRW 2,100 per 355 ml at 7-Eleven); Coca-Cola Zero Lemon Lime (launched 1 August 2026; aspartame, acesulfame-K, sucralose); Pepsi Zero Sugar Lime (KRW 2,100 at GS25); Woongjin The Victoria Cool Lime (lime and mint botanical craft soda, zero sugar and calorie, announced 22 June 2026).
- Closest botanical zero competitor: Woongjin Victoria Cool Lime. Its sweetener, can size and price are not disclosed.
- The remaining gap is narrow: a named botanical profile, no functional claim, and a sweetener-free or clearly stated formula.
- Market context (dated, not validated for 2025 to 2026): zero-sugar carbonates grew from KRW 92.4bn (2020) to KRW 368.3bn (2022) (aT via Korea Times). The MFDS sugar-zero category reached KRW 572.6bn in 2024, up 20.1%, and its product count rose from 261 to 590. Convenience stores report over half of carbonated sales as zero (GS25, January to April 2024, single retailer).

## Korea sweetener, label and claim rules

- MFDS (June 2025) confirms 22 permitted sweeteners, including acesulfame-K, sucralose and steviol glycosides. Beverage usage limits are not confirmed. A 2015 press figure (0.4 g per kg for sucralose in beverages) is the only numeric source found and is a proxy.
- Zero-sugar label (무당): under 0.5 g sugar per 100 g or ml. A sweetened product must state the sweetener. A calorie statement is required unless the product is under 20 kcal per 100 ml. In force from 1 January 2026.
- Functional claims: the general-food functional route lists 29 raw materials (for example ginseng, chlorella, propolis, guava leaf). No citrus is on that list, so the product cannot make a functional claim.
- Consumers: 68.6 percent of zero-soda drinkers in a 2024 Korea Consumer Agency survey assumed significantly fewer calories. The can should state sugar and calorie claims separately.

## Implications for the concept

1. Name the botanical as a whole extract and confirm its SFA status before the name is fixed (novel-food gate).
2. Choose the sweetener deliberately. A "no artificial sweetener" claim is available only with steviol or monk fruit, and the taste effect needs a test.
3. Keep label copy to taste, occasion and nutrient claims. Health and functional wording is barred in Singapore and not available in Korea.
4. Print a calorie statement and a sweetener declaration in Korea.
5. Cost in the possible Korean sugar levy as a sensitivity (the financial model does this).
