## 3. New items (verified or usable)

**Dissolvable and effervescent formats (novelty).**
- Waterdrop zero-sugar cubes: 20 markets, 2016 Vienna launch, 2021 US launch, per FoodNavigator (3 October 2024). Verified as a secondary read. The US date conflicts with a 2023 MediaPost report of retail US listings, so the timeline must be merged and reconciled.
- Ricola Drink Cubes, co-developed with Waterdrop (press release, 5 June 2025): sugar-free dissolvable cubes, rolled out in Austria and France from August 2025. This is a larger brand precedent than any cube in the earlier record.
- Incrediballs (New Zealand, February 2026): sugar-free effervescent drink tablets, using a co-crystal process that holds back the fizz until the tablet is in water. This is the closest in-market answer to "beyond a plain dissolvable cube": stabilisation is the innovation.
- Effervescent tablet research (2025 review; Delompré et al., Molecules 2025): tablets are highly moisture-sensitive, and bitter and metallic off-tastes drive negative perception. The off-taste result is directly relevant to a potassium-carbonate cube.
- Beverage solvent effect on tablets (2024): tablet disintegration is slower in milk than in water. The effect for a juice or soda base is unmeasured.

**Patents.**
- EP 4,516,115 A2 (Biofar, published 5 March 2025, pending): sodium-free effervescent carbonate using lactobionic acid, calcium carbonate or alternatives, and citric acid. Dependent claims add potassium hydrogen carbonate. This is a freedom-to-operate risk for the sodium-free route, and its legal status must be checked before any claim.
- US 2,851,361 (1958): dry effervescent concentrate for carbonated beverages, with at least 60% calcium carbonate. The register's first row said the claims cover compressed tablets with cold-water disintegration. The full claim text does not say that (only the specification does), so the claim scope is broader than the register stated.

**CO₂ and carbonation.**
- Essery (1952): CO₂ solubility in ice at −5 °C and −20 °C is about 1/20 and 1/100 of its solubility in water at 0 °C. It is a beer study and gives ratios only, so it supports a qualitative ice-exclusion argument, not a number for a cube.
- Henry's law for CO₂ in NaCl at sub-zero temperatures (2018): extrapolating above-zero constants to sub-zero temperatures underestimates dissolved CO₂. Relevant to any frozen variant, not to the dissolvable route.
- Frozen carbonated water at Europa-like conditions (2026): retention depends on freezing temperature, but the experiments are cryogenic and evacuated, so they do not transfer to a kitchen freezer.
- Carbonated sugar and ethanol solutions (2024): a critical review of CO₂ solubility models for sweetened drinks. It is the right source for sweetened solubility, but it is not yet read in full.
- Bottled carbonated water (2019): a minimum dissolved CO₂ is needed before bubbles can nucleate in a glass. This supports the claim that open-glass fizz depends on a threshold, not on a table value.
- NIST solubility database (SRD 106): tabulated CO₂ solubility in water from 0 to 25 °C. This should replace the 1967 Lange values as the citation for the screen's solubility constants.

**Sodium, potassium and electrolytes.**
- Potassium and blood pressure (Filippini et al., 2020): dose-response meta-analysis of 32 randomised trials. It supports a potassium-load discussion, but it does not give a safe limit for people with impaired potassium excretion.
- Carbonated and electrolyte-added water after mild dehydration (Takamata et al., 2026): 15 young adults. A small study, but it is directly about carbonated water and hydration.
- Sodium and glycerol solutions for fluid balance (Baker et al., 2025): randomised, 37 adults. It shows that low sodium concentrations change fluid retention.
- Mineral content of zero-labelled beverages (Choi et al., 2026): 86 processed beverages in matched pairs. Carbonated zero-labelled drinks had higher potassium (12.17 vs 6.73 mg per 100 g). This is relevant to the potassium route, but it is an abstract-level read.

**Thai regulation.**
- Thai FDA news (14 February 2024): Notifications 445 (nutrition labels), 446 (GDA), 447 (health claims) and 448 (supplements) were published on 4 January 2024 and took effect on 2 July 2024. Selling of old stock is exempt until 1 July 2027. Verified against the FDA page.
- **Conflict on the sodium thresholds.** The regulation track read a Thai FDA English translation of Notification 445 and reported "sodium free below 5 mg", "very low below 35 mg" and "low below 140 mg" per reference serving. The register's critic says the claim thresholds sit in Attachment 4, which was not read, and that the 140 mg and 5 mg figures match the US rule (21 CFR 101.61). Until the Thai text is read, the Thai sodium thresholds stay unverified. [rework/45](45_working_candidate_rework.md) has been corrected to say so.
- ChemLinked gives three different effective dates for Notification 445. The Thai FDA primary text should be used instead.

**Thai consumer.**
- Thiboonboon et al. (2024, Public Health): daily sugar-sweetened beverage consumption in the 2021 Health Behaviour survey, varying by socioeconomic status. This is the most relevant Thai peer-reviewed source for the 45–59 analysis, but its age-band tables are not published.
- Phulkerd et al. (2020, Nutrients): consumption fell after the 2017 sugar tax in a cohort of 5,594 Thai residents. It is a strong causal-design source.
- Phulkerd et al. (2022, Food Policy): carbonated drinks were the most consumed taxed drink in a 2019 survey of 4,736 people.
- Loyfah et al. (2025, PLOS One): artificially sweetened drinks in urban educated adults, from the 2021 survey.
- Talek et al. (2025): alcohol-free mock beverages among Muslim youths in the Deep South (n = 407). Relevant to the alcohol-alternative occasion, but a narrow population.
- Mintel press release (16 February 2026): 44% trying to strictly reduce sugar, 67% plan to cut sweet drinks, and 61% plan to reduce sugary ready-to-drink drinks. Stated intention, all ages, unverified.

**Markets and packaging.**
- Life-cycle assessment of beverage packaging in Thailand (2024): cans, aseptic cartons, PET and glass compared. It recommends end-of-life recycling and recycled-content policies first.
- Aluminium can sales in Thailand (AL Circle, June 2026): 679 million cans sold in Q1 2026 by one producer. A volume signal, not a price.
- Thai anti-dumping probe on PET resin from China (March 2026): a price risk for the packaging survey's PET route. Unverified (a blog read).

## 4. Contradictions the critic found

- **Sodium thresholds:** the regulation track verified them from an English translation; the register says they are unverified. rework/45 now carries the conflict.
- **Waterdrop US entry:** 2021 (FoodNavigator) against 2023 retail listings (MediaPost). Both are marked verified. The register does not show whether they are online and retail dates.
- **US 2,851,361:** the register first described claims that the full claims text does not contain.
- **Mintel and the menopause cluster:** the Mintel women's wellness page is cited in the soy-candidate rows but shows no figures. Those rows stay out of the lime-cube evidence base.
- **Thai youth drinking:** the 9.6% and 24.8% figures come from unlabelled or unreadable sources. They must not be cited.
