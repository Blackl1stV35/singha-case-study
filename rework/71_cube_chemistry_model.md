# 71. Cube chemistry model (T8): taste, dissolution time and fizz duration

Status at 20 October 2026, revised twice the same day after two independent verifier reviews (section 14 lists every issue and what was done). Desk models only. Product: Singha Lemon Soda Cube, one cube in 330 mL of water (potassium bicarbonate 0.81 g, citric acid 2.10 g, sucralose 33 mg today and 55 mg proposed, binder and filler 0.80 g, lemon flavour 0.05 g; 14 mm cube; central dry mass 3.79 g). ## 1. Result in brief

1. Taste (chemistry only, no taste result claimed). The equilibrium pH at serve is about 3.4 (3.47 at 5 C, 3.42 at 20 C, 3.40 at 30 C; ionic strength 0.027 mol/L counted). The working pH of 3.5 therefore stands within about 0.1: with every activity coefficient set to 1 the model gives 3.51 at 20 C (3.50 at 25 C), and about 1.97 g of citric acid would give exactly 3.50 at 20 C. The cube carries more acid than the lemon-lime benchmark (Sprite) on every basis, but the size of the gap depends on which Sprite sample is used: buffered acid to pH 7 is 1.33 times the Serbian Sprite titration on the degassed basis (1.72 with all CO2 retained, an upper edge), and about 4.0 times (5.2 with all CO2) the acid that the citric acid assay of a Peshawar Sprite sample (1.2 g/L) can supply; by citric acid mass the cube holds 5.3 times as much. Sweetness is 6.0 g sugar equivalent per 100 mL at 33 mg (4.0 to 7.0 over the potency range) against about 10 g for Sprite; 55 mg matches 10 g. Potassium is 24.5 mM, about 10 times the potassium chloride detection threshold in water, and 3 to 8 times a combined caffeine and potassium chloride bitterness recognition range in water (a proxy). CO2 is 1.08 g/L, below the 2.0 g/L at which a wine mouthfeel difference began and about 14 percent of Sprite's 7.5 g/L. The chemistry predicts a drink that carries more acid, is thinner in sweetness and is weaker in bite than the benchmark, with a potassium note that is not quantified. Sensory data are absent.
2. Dissolution time (model estimate, not a measurement). At 25 C and a central set of assumed inputs the cube dissolves in about 6 minutes if stirred (347 s) and about 11 minutes if not stirred (645 s, at an assumed flow of 0.5 mm/s), on condition that it breaks up into fine pieces. Across all the assumed ranges the 5th to 95th percentile is 3 to 22 minutes stirred and 5 to 74 minutes not stirred (the medians are 9.6 and 16.4 minutes; assumed ranges, not a probability). The unstirred time rests on an assumed flow. A derived bracket for the central fragments gives about 5 to 6 minutes for gas-driven and density-driven flow (close to the stirred case) and about 24 minutes for no flow at all. Treating the whole cube as dissolving at a composition-weighted saturation concentration (517 to 601 g/L) instead of the potassium bicarbonate value (224 g/L) shortens the central times to about 4.5 minutes stirred and 6 to 7 minutes unstirred. The most favourable case is about 76 seconds. An intact cube, with no break-up, would take about 3.0 hours stirred and about 12 hours unstirred. The time is set by the size of the pieces after break-up (dominant uncertainty), not by solubility or reaction rate.
3. Fizz duration (surface loss only, model estimate). The earlier 96 minutes reproduces exactly (96.0 minutes at 25 C, density basis, 10 second surface renewal), but it is a stirred-surface value and is not an upper bound. At 20 C, with the full 0.55 volume dose dissolved at serve and loss through the free surface only, the drink stays above 0.44 g/L (a mineral-water proxy) for about 1.7 hours (assumed 10 second surface renewal) to 3.4 hours (calm-lake coefficient). The short end is an assumed renewal time and the long end a lake coefficient; neither is a glass measurement. The lowest coefficient in the sources read (0.65 cm/h, one pond, floating chambers) gives about 14 hours, so no sourced upper bound exists. Bubble nucleation can shorten all of these; a champagne proxy gives a floor of about 15 minutes, and no central time with nucleation is stated. The share of the dose still dissolved at serve (retention) is unmeasured and decisive: the still-glass time stays above 150 minutes only while retention is at least 0.79, falls to 46 minutes at 0.5 and 22 minutes at 0.45, and is zero below 0.41. Surface loss during dissolution alone leaves 0.91 or more of the dose for dissolution times up to about 11 minutes, but that is an upper bound on retention because bubbling at the cube and on the glass is not bounded by any source.
4. Robust conclusions (one-at-a-time tornados in section 7): the pH sits between 3.3 and 3.55 for every single input change tested; the buffered acid is above Sprite's in every change tested (1.15 to 1.51 times, degassed) and above the 1.2 flag rule in all but citric acid minus 10 percent; sweetness at 33 mg is below the 0.8 rule in every change; potassium is above 9 times the chloride threshold in every change; no nucleation occurs on the pure-CO2 criterion at 0.55 volumes between 5 and 30 C; an intact cube takes hours; the fragment size has the largest swing in the dissolution time. The surface-only fizz time is robust only conditionally: it stays above 150 minutes under every single change tested only while retention is at least 0.79 (with the retention range extended to 0.4 to 1.0 it is not robust, and retention is the largest unmeasured input). Not robust: any single dissolution time, the unstirred time (assumed flow), whether nucleation occurs (it depends on dissolved air, which is not sourced), and the size of the acid gap against Sprite (it depends on the Sprite sample).

## 4. Model a: taste chemistry (cube_chemistry_taste.py)

Method. Charge balance (K+ + H+ = OH- + HCO3- + 2 CO3(2-) + citrate charge) solved for the operational pH (the activity pH a meter reads). Citric acid constants come from the Goldberg relation at each temperature. Apparent constants in terms of concentrations are pK' = pK + log gamma_A - log gamma_HA, with Davies activity coefficients at the ionic strength of the drink (solved by iteration). Because the anion is more highly charged than the acid, the apparent pKs fall as ionic strength rises (at 20 C and I = 0.0265: 3.075, 4.571 and 6.058 against 3.128, 4.761 and 6.396 at I = 0; at I = 0.1 the third pK is 5.87). Hydrogen ion concentration is a_H divided by gamma_H. Titratable acidity is the sodium base needed to move the drink from its pH to 5.5, 7.0 and 8.2, with and without retained CO2; the ionic strength is iterated at the end point (0.13 to 0.22 mol/L, counted, Davies still inside its stated range); the variant with the ionic strength frozen at the start is also reported (it lowers the totals by 2 to 3 percent). Sweetness is sucralose times potency over the serve. The benchmark for a lemon-lime soda is Sprite.

Recipe basis (DERIVED): CO2 generated 8.091 mmol per serve, which is 0.5495 volumes and 1.079 g/L (exactly 0.55 volumes is 1.080 g/L; the difference is rounding of the design dose). Potassium 24.5 mM (316 mg per serve; 959 mg/L). Total citric acid 33.1 mM (6.36 g/L). Stoichiometric citric acid for the CO2 is 0.518 g, leaving 1.58 g free per serve (4.79 g/L).

Basis of the acid comparison. Barac et al. titrated 50 mL of Sprite opened and stirred in a beaker; the paper does not say the sample was degassed or report CO2 loss. Sprite at 3.8 volumes holds about 170 mmol/L of CO2, which alone would need about 138 mmol/L of base to reach pH 7, yet the measured total is 54.8 mmol/L, so most of the CO2 had left during the titration (INFERENCE, not stated by the paper). The like-for-like basis for the Serbian sample is therefore the degassed cube (1.33); the all-CO2-retained value (1.72) is an upper edge. The two Sprite samples also disagree on how much acid Sprite holds: the Serbian titration (54.8 mmol/L, 3.5 g/L citric equivalent) is about 3 times what the Peshawar citric assay (1.2 g/L citric acid) can supply as acid alone (18.1 mmol/L), and the model's pH for that citric acid alone (2.75) is close to the measured 2.71, which is consistent with most of the Serbian total being carbonic acid (a consistency check, not proof; Sprite also lists sodium citrate on the Serbian label). If the Peshawar assay is the better guide, the cube carries about 4 times Sprite's acid; if the Serbian total is all acid, 1.33 times. Both are single bottlings, so the ratio is bracketed from about 1.3 to about 4 (5 by citric mass), not fixed. In every comparison the cube carries more acid.

Sucralose needed to match a sugar equivalent (per 330 mL, DERIVED): 47 mg for 8.6 g per 100 mL; 55 mg for 10.0 g; 64 mg for 11.7 g (potency 600). For 10.0 g the range is 47 mg (potency 700) to 83 mg (potency 400). The 300 mg/kg limit allows 99 mg per serve (300 mg/kg x 0.330 kg). 55 mg is about 167 mg/kg, against about 100 mg/kg at 33 mg.

Structure. Nucleation check. Two criteria were examined. (N1) The pure-CO2 criterion used by the Liger-Belair group for beer and champagne: bubbles form only when the dissolved CO2 exceeds H (P_amb + 2 sigma / r). The published radii are quoted to one figure, so agreement is to about 20 percent at best, and the rule is used as the literature's criterion, not as a validated predictor. At 0.55 volumes the flat limit is 1.69 g/L at 20 C (1.29 at 30 C; 2.62 at 5 C), and even a 1 micrometre site needs 3.25 g/L. So on this criterion there is no nucleation between 5 and 30 C. (N2) The air-inclusive criterion: if the drink holds dissolved air at equilibrium with the atmosphere (about 1 atm of air in the liquid), a CO2 partial pressure of 0.65 bar at 20 C exceeds the Laplace pressure for any air-filled cavity larger than 1.45 micrometres. Sites of 1 to 10 micrometres are then partly active, and the largest sites would drive the drink to about 0.16 g/L at 20 C (0.12 at 30 C; 0.24 at 5 C), below the threshold. The air content of the drink and the density of active sites are not sourced, so N2 cannot be ruled in or out from the sources read.
