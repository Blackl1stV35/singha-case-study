## Figures and statements (short fragments, our paraphrase around them)

- Data: Nielsen Homescan Consumer Panel retail purchases, US households (Alaska and Hawaii excluded), 2004 to 2019; regression sample n = 6,908,936; restaurant meals excluded. Temperature from NOAA GSOD stations within 100 km of the household county.
- Model: household fixed-effects regression; the main specification uses 2 C temperature bins; a segmented regression has breaks at 12 C and 30 C.
- Headline: added sugar consumption is "positively related to temperature, notably within 12-30 C at a rate of 0.70 g C-1". Unit: grams per degree Celsius per capita-day, adjusted to adult male equivalents.
- Sweetened beverages: the main driver, rising "sharply between 12 C and 30 C (added sugar, 0.73 g C-1)".
- Baseline quoted from outside the sample: individuals aged 2 and over consumed "over 70 g (17 teaspoons) per day in 2017-2018". The article gives no sample mean for added sugar from sweetened beverages and no percentage per degree.
- Above 30 C: the binned model shows "plateauing or even declining beyond 30 C", which the authors say should be read with caution; only "0.8% of observations go beyond 30 C"; the Discussion says the response slows above 30 C.
- Climate zones: "minimal response in the Hot-Humid Climate Zone"; largest in the Marine zone; the authors state the differences are not statistically significant. No explicit hot-versus-cool test; cooling degree days show no significant difference across regions.
- Race and ethnicity: "The Asian group shows a nonsignificant reaction" (variations across groups are substantial).
- Age and sex: age of the male and female household heads is a control only; no age-group result is reported. The effects are "consistent across male and female heads".
- Correction: an Author Correction was published on 10 June 2026. It was not read, and what it changes is unknown.

## DERIVED (our arithmetic, flagged)

- 0.70 g per C divided by 70 g per day is about 1.0 percent per C of total added sugar. This mixes an in-sample slope with an outside baseline and is for scale only. It is not a sales elasticity and is not applied to soft drinks alone.
