## Thresholds (p. 6)
Hyperkalaemia is mild at serum potassium 5.5 to 5.9 mmol/L, moderate at 6.0 to 6.4 and severe at 6.5 or more.

## Drugs that add to the risk (the part that matters for a potassium salt taken by people on these drugs)

### Table 6, page 35: "Drugs that pose an additive effect on risk of hyperkalaemia in patients receiving RAASi and MRAs"
The list under the heading "Drugs that potentiate risk of hyperkalaemia in patients receiving RAASi or MRAs" is: trimethoprim or co-trimoxazole; potassium sparing diuretics; potassium supplements; NSAIDs; non-selective beta-blockers; salt substitutes (lo-salt); digoxin toxicity.
(RAASi are ACE inhibitors and angiotensin receptor blockers; MRAs are mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists such as spironolactone and eplerenone. The page 55 table names them as "RAASi (ACE Inhibitors, Angiotensin II Receptor Blockers, Mineralocorticoid Receptor Antagonists)".)

### Table 9, page 55: "Drugs implicated in development of hyperkalaemia and exacerbating factors"
Drugs: RAASi; potassium supplements; potassium-sparing diuretics; trimethoprim or co-trimoxazole; NSAIDs; non-selective beta-blockers. Risk of hyperkalaemia is "increased in": renal impairment, diabetes mellitus, the elderly, use of more than one RAASi drug, and combining any of these groups of drugs.

### Table 32, page 122: "Drugs commonly associate with hyperkalaemia and mechanisms"
- Affect aldosterone secretion: ACE inhibitors; angiotensin receptor blockers; NSAIDs; calcineurin inhibitors; heparins; antifungals (ketoconazole, fluconazole, itraconazole).
- Block aldosterone binding (MRA): spironolactone, eplerenone, finerenone.
- Inhibit the epithelial sodium channel: potassium-sparing diuretics (amiloride, triamterene); trimethoprim or co-trimoxazole; pentamidine.
- Alter transmembrane potassium movement: beta-blockers (atenolol, metoprolol, propranolol); digoxin; intravenous cationic amino acids; hyperosmolar solutions; suxamethonium.
- Potassium-containing agents: potassium supplements (examples named: Sando-K, Kay-Cee-L liquid); salt substitutes; herbal medicines (alfalfa, dandelion, horsetail, milkweed, nettle); stored red blood cells.

### Other statements on drugs
- Page 33: about 20% of patients with moderate to severe CKD develop hyperkalaemia (serum potassium above 5.5 mmol/L), and the 2019 Renal Association and British Society for Heart Failure guidance advises stopping drugs that potentiate hyperkalaemia ("K+ supplements, Trimethoprim, NSAIDs, amiloride") when potassium is 5.5 or more.
- Guideline 11.2 (pages 54 to 56): caution in prescribing trimethoprim to patients with renal impairment or taking RAASi drugs (grade 1A); trimethoprim to be avoided in patients receiving RAASi drugs.
- Page 123: "Avoid drug combinations that potentiate hyperkalaemia (e.g. ACE-I and Trimethoprim)"; for CKD stages 4 and 5, seek specialist dietary advice.

## Dietary potassium (page 37)
- "Dietary advice in adults without kidney disease": the WHO recommends an average potassium intake of about 3.9 g a day (100 mmol); the Institute of Medicine recommends 4.7 g a day. A potassium-rich diet is "not recommended in patients with advanced CKD or ESRD".
- "Dietary advice in patients with CKD": the historical low-potassium diet is defined as 2 to 3 g a day (51 to 77 mmol a day). The guideline says there is a lack of strong evidence for this approach and that a blanket policy is not recommended; excessive restriction may risk cardiovascular disease and malnutrition. For CKD with persistent hyperkalaemia (serum potassium 5.5 mmol/L or more) a restriction below 3 g a day (below 77 mmol) or 1 mmol per kg ideal body weight is recommended.
- Processed foods containing potassium additives are "a hidden source" of dietary potassium and should be limited (page 37, citing KDIGO 2023 for a diverse diet with more plant foods and less ultra-processed food).
- Page 38: KDIGO (2023) advises registered dietitians or accredited nutrition providers for patients with CKD G3 to G5 and emerging hyperkalaemia. The "low potassium diet" implies one regimen for all, which the guideline says can be restrictive; an individualised approach by a specialist dietitian is preferred.

## What this adds to T10
- It is a current UK primary guideline that lists potassium supplements and salt substitutes together with trimethoprim, potassium-sparing diuretics, NSAIDs, non-selective beta-blockers and digoxin toxicity as drugs or agents that raise the hyperkalaemia risk in people on ACE inhibitors, ARBs or MRAs. A dissolved potassium salt in a drink is a potassium-containing agent of the same kind as a supplement or salt substitute (our reading; the guideline does not mention drinks or cubes).
- It gives a second numeric diet figure: 2 to 3 g a day as the historical low-potassium diet, and below 3 g a day for persistent hyperkalaemia. One serve (316.6 mg) is 10.6% of 3,000 mg and 15.8% of 2,000 mg. The Thai 1,500 to 2,000 mg is lower than both UK figures.
- It names no milligram threshold for a single potassium salt dose.
