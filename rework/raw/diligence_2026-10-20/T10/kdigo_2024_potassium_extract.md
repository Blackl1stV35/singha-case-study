## Section 3.11 Hyperkalemia in CKD (page S224 onward)
- "Hyperkalemia is uncommon when the eGFR is >60 ml/min per 1.73 m2 and increases with lower GFR."
- Adults with CKD G3, A1 in general and high-risk population cohorts (CKD-PC) had an adjusted prevalence of hyperkalaemia (serum potassium above 5.0 mmol/l) of 8.8% with diabetes and 4.5% without diabetes, rising to 34.4% and 23.7% at CKD G5, A3 (Figure 30). Hyperkalaemia is not inevitable at lower eGFR.
- Figure 32, actions for potassium above 5.5 mmol/l: first line address correctable factors (review non-RASi medicines such as NSAIDs and trimethoprim; assess dietary potassium intake); second line medicines; third line last resort.

## Table 26, page S226: medications associated with increased risk of hyperkalemia (class, mechanism, example)
- ACEi: captopril, lisinopril, perindopril.
- ARB: losartan, irbesartan, candesartan.
- Aldosterone antagonist: spironolactone, eplerenone, finerenone.
- Beta-adrenergic receptor blocker: propranolol, metoprolol, atenolol.
- Digitalis glycoside: digoxin.
- Heparin: heparin sodium (reduced aldosterone production).
- Potassium-sparing diuretic: amiloride, triamterene.
- NSAIDs: ibuprofen, naproxen, diclofenac.
- Calcineurin inhibitors: cyclosporine, tacrolimus.
- ns-MRA: finerenone.
- Other: trimethoprim and pentamidine.
(Data from Weiner et al. and the KDIGO Diabetes work group, as stated under the table.)

## Section 3.11.5 Dietary considerations (page S227)
- Text: in early CKD, high intake of foods naturally rich in potassium appears protective against progression; restriction of fruit and vegetables "may be harmful" to cardiac health and is not endorsed.
- Practice Point 3.11.5.1: individualised approach for CKD G3 to G5 with emergent hyperkalaemia, dietary and pharmacological, with a renal dietitian.
- Practice Point 3.11.5.2: "Provide advice to limit the intake of foods rich in bioavailable potassium (e.g., processed foods) for people with CKD G3-G5 who have a history of hyperkalemia or as a prevention strategy during disease periods in which hyperkalemia risk may be a concern."
- Text: highly processed foods rich in potassium additives, meats, dairy, juices and salt substitutes made with potassium chloride are higher in absorbable potassium than many fresh plant foods.
- Figure 33 (data from Picard et al., J Ren Nutr 2021;31:210-214): absorption of potassium from plant foods 50% to 60%; animal foods 70% to 90%; processed foods, potassium salts "reported to be 90%".
- Practice Points 3.6.2 and 3.6.3 (PDF page 44 of the 199-page file), verbatim from the text layer re-read on 20 October 2026: "Practice Point 3.6.2: Changes in BP, serum creatinine, and serum potassium should be checked within 2-4 weeks of initiation or increase in the dose of a RASi, depending on the current GFR and serum potassium." and "Practice Point 3.6.3: Hyperkalemia associated with use of RASi can often be managed by measures to reduce the serum potassium levels rather than decreasing the dose or stopping RASi." (RASi is KDIGO's abbreviation for renin-angiotensin system inhibitors, ACE inhibitors or ARBs.) Practice Point 3.8.3: check potassium before and after a non-steroidal MRA.
- What the "measures to reduce the serum potassium levels" are, in KDIGO's own figures (re-read 20 October 2026): Figure 21 (algorithm for monitoring potassium and eGFR after starting RAS inhibitors; page S214 of the journal, PDF page 99), the hyperkalaemia branch: "Review concurrent drugs", "Moderate dietary potassium intake", "Consider: diuretics, sodium bicarbonate, potassium binders", then "Reduce dose or stop ACEi or ARB if mitigation strategies ineffective". Figure 32 (PDF page 113), first line, "Address correctable factors": "Review non-RASi medications (e.g. NSAIDs, trimethoprim)" and "Assess dietary potassium intake (dietary referral) and consider appropriate moderation of dietary potassium intake". So the guideline's way of keeping a patient on an ACE inhibitor or ARB when potassium is high includes moderating dietary potassium. That is advice to lower potassium, which supports a precaution for people on these drugs; it is not a statement about a drink or a daily milligram figure.
- Food labelling (PDF page 113, verbatim): "...educating people with CKD and healthcare providers, using clear messaging, on dietary approaches to potassium management is needed (https://www.theisn.org/initiatives/toolkits/raasi-toolkit/...), as well as a policy to improve food labeling by detailing the added potassium used in processing." This is a call for a policy in guideline text, not a rule.

## Scope of the 90 percent figure
- The 90 percent in Figure 33 is KDIGO's reported absorption of potassium salts that are added to processed foods (via Picard et al., J Ren Nutr 2021). It was not measured for this cube. Applying it to the dissolved potassium bicarbonate and citrate of the cube is the team's extrapolation.

## What it says about a numeric daily ceiling
- None. KDIGO 2024 gives no mg per day potassium limit; it asks for individualised dietary advice from a dietitian once hyperkalaemia has occurred or is a concern.
