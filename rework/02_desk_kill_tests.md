Needs the Notification 92 text and a registration adviser.

## B. Midlife women standardised-isoflavone unsweetened soy drink

1. **Food or supplement: pass, on public text.** Notification No. 293 (B.E. 2548) cl. 2 defines a supplement by a non-conventional form (tablet, capsule, powder). A 250 ml UHT drink falls outside it and sits on the food route. Caveats: the consolidated texts of 405, 411 and 448 were not retrieved. The registration route for a food with added isoflavone extract is not settled. A vendor page says "specifically controlled food"; not checked against a primary text.
2. **Menopause or hot-flush claim: unresolved, the decisive check.** Notification No. 447 (B.E. 2566) lists no soy, isoflavone, menopause, hot-flush or sleep claim, so nothing bars it outright. A route exists under cl. 5: assessment by a Thai FDA-recognised centre, then FDA approval. The open risk is cl. 6(2), which bars claims misleading consumers about the "treatment, relief, cure or prevention of disease". The text does not say whether hot flushes or sleep problems count as a disease. Cl. 2(3) also requires well-designed human studies in the target group.
3. **Comparable drink already sold: unresolved.** None found for menopause or hot flushes. The audit is incomplete: Lazada's two Thai searches returned "no results"; Shopee redirected to a traffic-verification page, which was not bypassed; Makro, Tops and Boots routes returned 404; Watsons showed no product list. The Thai FDA product database was not queried.
4. **Dose guidance: unresolved.** No binding Thai, EFSA or Singapore cap found. EFSA's 2015 press release found no harm at supplement-level doses over up to a year, but set no tolerable upper limit and excluded women in transition to menopause and women with a cancer history, which is part of the target group. A Mahidol pharmacy page (undated, non-binding) gives 50 to 100 mg a day as a guide. A 2018 Chiang Mai study found 25.5 to 63.5 mg per 250 ml across four Thai soy-milk brands, so a labelled dose needs the powder's certificate of analysis and a lot-testing plan.

Not settled: consumer demand; dose safety in the target group; certificate of analysis.

2. **Alcohol-advertising rules: unresolved, not barred outright.** Under the amended Alcohol Control Act (No. 2, B.E. 2568), s.32/3 bars indirect advertising. That includes using alcohol brand names, logos or symbols on a non-alcoholic product where it could reasonably be seen as promoting alcohol. An alcohol-alternative position is not barred on its face. The test depends on the facts. Gaps: the Royal Gazette text could not be read, so the wording comes from secondary summaries. The Ministerial conditions under s.32/1 were still being drafted (a public consultation was reported for 9 September 2026). The Act defines no alcohol percentage, so whether a near-zero product falls within it is unverified. The effective date is 8 November 2025 per a law-firm page; one Thai excerpt says 90 days.

### Follow-up search: Thai evidence for drinking less (18 October 2026)

The file's case for C rests on sober-curious adults as a trend. The follow-up search looked for Thai sources showing young adults drinking less. None holds up.

- **The youth-decline headline does not reproduce.** Thai news (Naewna, Thansettakij) reports that drinking among ages 15 to 24 fell from 34.7 percent (2021) to 24.8 percent (2024), citing the NSO ([Naewna](https://www.naewna.com/likesara/907169)). The NSO's own Social Indicators 2025, Table 4.6.2 ([NSO](https://www.nso.go.th/public/e-book/Indicators-Social/Social-Indicators-2025/284/)), does not reproduce it. As parsed from the page, the 15 to 19 figures went from 9.0 to 9.6 percent and the 20 to 24 figures from 31.6 to 37.8 percent, between 2021 and 2024. Every sex and age sub-group shown rose. A simple average of the two 2021 sub-groups is about 20 percent, not 34.7. The table text was jumbled and parsed by a tool. The Statistical Yearbook 2025 chart ([NSO](https://www.nso.go.th/public/e-book/Statistical-Yearbook/SYB-2025/228/)) could not be read. This stays unverified until someone checks the printed table.
- **The national figure rose.** The NSO share of people aged 15 and over who drink went from 28.0 percent (2021) to 35.2 percent (2024), about 20.9 million people. [The Nation (12 June 2025)](https://www.nationthailand.com/health-wellness/40051164), citing NSO data through NESDC, calls it "a notable rise". The NSO table gives the same totals.
- **Per-capita consumption fell over the long run.** The Alcohol Research Center puts consumption at about 6 litres of pure alcohol per person per year in 2023 to 2024, down from over 8 litres before 2007 ([Dailynews, 21 July 2026](https://www.dailynews.co.th/news/6044943/)). No method is given. A per-capita measure over the long run does not contradict the rise in prevalence above.
- **Buddhist Lent campaign.** The Alcohol Research Center's evaluation of the 2025 alcohol-free Lent campaign reports 8.6 million people (32.3 percent) who reduced or quit drinking during Lent. No sample or method is given. It is event-specific.
- **Bangkok youth survey (July 2025).** A youth network surveyed 2,200 people in Bangkok and its vicinity. 34.3 percent had never drunk (a lifetime measure), and 72.3 percent said alcohol-free activities should be offered during Lent. The age split is not quoted here. This is an advocacy network's own survey, and sampling is not stated.
- **Thai demand for alternatives (vendor, 2023).** [GlobalData's Q1 2023 Thai survey](https://www.globaldata.com/media/consumer/innovative-product-positioning-drive-demand-low-no-alcoholic-drinks-thailand-says-globaldata/) (n = 508): 81 percent consume low- or zero-alcohol alternatives at least weekly; 62 percent have stopped buying alcoholic drinks because of cost; 31 percent drink regularly. Vendor-run, 2023, all consumers, not split by age. This is the strongest Thai demand signal we have, and its stated reason is price, not sober-curiosity.
- **Not Thai.** The Gen Z moderation survey in [Marketeer (24 April 2024)](https://www.marketeeronline.co/archives/350376) is Australian (1,000 respondents). The 40 percent Gen Z limits-alcohol figure is Mintel UK. Mintel's 2023 Thailand finding is a preference for sweet non-alcoholic drinks, not a reduction in drinking.

**Reading for C's consumer case.** No Thai source shows that young adults are drinking less, and the national data point the other way. What the Thai data supports is demand for alcohol alternatives, driven by price (62 percent) and by alcohol-free occasions (Lent). That is not the evening sober-curious reason the file gives. Proposal: score C's consumer case at 0 for the current framing. A reframed concept, aimed at price-led switchers or alcohol-free occasions, could support 1, but that is a different target and needs its own test.
