# T10: potassium, potassium-retaining medicines and kidney disease: screening of the 317 mg serve (20 October 2026, fourth pass after the third verification)

Status: desk screening for a medical reviewer at Gate 2. It is not medical advice and it does not clear the serve. Team record. The slide text in section 9 and the wording in section 8 are written to be judge-safe. Every figure carries a tier (P primary, S secondary, DERIVED, ASSUMPTION, PROXY, UNVERIFIED), a source and the date read (20 October 2026). The page text behind each sourced figure is saved in `rework/raw/diligence_2026-10-20/T10/`. All arithmetic comes from `rework/screen/t10_potassium_cover.py`, with results in `rework/screen/t10_potassium_cover_results.json` (deterministic; run with `python -I -X utf8`; two runs give the same checksum). ## 1. Bottom line

1. **The serve.** One serve holds 316.6 mg of potassium (8.1 mmol, from 0.811 g of potassium bicarbonate; DERIVED). 316.6 mg is the handover value and the base of every share in this record; the script's own recomputation from 0.811 g gives 316.7 mg, which rounds to the same 317 mg and gives the same shares to one decimal. That is 9.0% of the Thai reference of 3,500 mg a day (9.046%, DERIVED). The reference is primary: Notification of the Ministry of Public Health No. 445 B.E. 2566, Annex 3 row 32, in the English translation published by the Thai FDA (P; the Thai Gazette text was not opened). A printed Thai nutrition panel would show 320 mg under the rounding rule in Annex 1.
2. **The medicines.** KDIGO 2024, Table 26 (P), lists the classes that raise blood potassium: ACE inhibitors, ARBs, aldosterone antagonists (spironolactone, eplerenone, finerenone), potassium-sparing diuretics (amiloride, triamterene), trimethoprim and pentamidine, heparin, NSAIDs, calcineurin inhibitors, beta blockers and digoxin. The UK Kidney Association guideline of October 2023 (P) lists potassium supplements and salt substitutes together with trimethoprim, potassium-sparing diuretics, NSAIDs, non-selective beta blockers and digoxin toxicity as drugs that add to the hyperkalaemia risk of patients on ACE inhibitors, ARBs or mineralocorticoid antagonists (Tables 6, 9 and 32). US labels read as SPL text on DailyMed (P) treat potassium supplements and potassium salt substitutes as add-on risks in two ways. The Zestril label (lisinopril) names them as risk factors for hyperkalaemia (section 5.5), and its section 17 tells prescribers to counsel patients not to use potassium-containing salt substitutes without consulting the physician. The Cozaar (losartan), Inspra (eplerenone) and Aldactone (spironolactone) labels in section 17, and the amiloride label in its contraindications text, advise patients to avoid, or to ask the prescriber before using, potassium supplements and salt substitutes (amiloride: not to be used except in severe or refractory hypokalaemia, which is prescriber-directed use). The sulfamethoxazole and trimethoprim label is in this record for its own hyperkalaemia statement only; it does not mention potassium supplements or salt substitutes (searched, zero occurrences). None of the labels read, and none of the guidelines read, sets a milligram threshold below which a potassium salt is safe for these patients.
3. **The US line.** 21 CFR 201.306 sets its lines at 100 mg of potassium per tablet or unit and 20 mg per mL for liquids (P; read in the official CFR 2014 print and in the eCFR current text, which carry the same lines and the same amendment note ending 1 February 2002). The 99 mg figure in the brief is not in that text. The Council for Responsible Nutrition (a trade body, S, undated page) says the 99 mg per dose limit is specific to over-the-counter drugs, that FDA has not ruled on potassium in dietary supplements, and that makers have followed the limit in practice. So 99 mg is an over-the-counter drug labelling convention just under the 100 mg line that supplement makers follow, and the comparator kept as primary is the 100 mg line. The serve is 3.17 times that line (DERIVED). Whether a dissolvable food cube falls within the section is a question for a U.S. regulatory reader and is not settled here.
4. **The kidney guidance.** The Thai nutrition guideline for adults with kidney disease (Clinical Practice Recommendation for Nutritional Management in Adult Kidney Patients, B.E. 2561, updated March B.E. 2563, Thai Society for Parenteral and Enteral Nutrition with the Nephrology Society of Thailand) recommends in recommendation 4.4.3 (page 27) a low-potassium diet of not more than 1,500 to 2,000 mg a day for chronic kidney disease before dialysis. Its explanation (page 28) applies that limit when blood potassium is high and warns that over-restricting potassium may cost foods that are good for the heart (P; Thai text read, translation ours). The guideline gives no figure by kidney disease stage. KDIGO 2024 (P) sets no daily milligram figure. Two secondary reviews (S: Molina et al., Frontiers in Medicine 2021; Lambert et al., Nephrology 2022) report the same for KDOQI 2020, whose own text returned HTTP 403 and was not opened. The UK Kidney Association (P) describes the historical low-potassium diet as 2 to 3 g a day, does not recommend a blanket restriction, and recommends below 3 g a day for persistent serum potassium of 5.5 mmol/L or more. For a patient on the Thai limit one serve is 15.8% to 21.1% of the day; against 3,000 mg it is 10.6% (DERIVED). These ceilings apply to a whole day of food and drink for patients on such a limit. They are not per-serve limits and do not describe the general population.
5. **Coverage (DERIVED, overlaps handled, not double counted).** The narrow group is kidney disease at stage 3 to 5, or treatment for high blood pressure with an ACE inhibitor or an ARB. The model puts it at about 1.1% of men aged 25 to 34 (model bounds 0.1% to 5.5%), about 4.7% of men aged 25 to 59 (2.2% to 13.0%) and about 13.0% of adults aged 25 and over (5.2% to 25.4%), which is about 6.1 million people (2.4 to 11.9 million). The bounds are the extremes of our own assumptions (every input at its low value with full overlap, and every input at its high value with no overlap). They are not a statistical interval, and the central value is a midpoint of assumed bounds. The kidney survey alone does not explain the width: with the 2014 survey only, men aged 25 to 34 are 0.14 / 1.07 / 3.30 and adults 5.21 / 13.02 / 19.40, and the 2004 survey adds 2.2 points at the top for men aged 25 to 34 and 6.0 for adults. Section 5.3 names the other drivers. The population weights were corrected in this pass (age 60 had been counted twice); with the duplicate removed from the other band the adult value would be 12.5% (5.8 million), so the adult central value is 12.5% to 13.0% on the two ways of removing it (section 5.2). Adding all diabetes and all treated high blood pressure widens the group to 5.1% of men aged 25 to 34 (1.9% to 11.9%) and 27.3% of adults aged 25 and over (13.9% to 44.1%). About three quarters of the adult central value comes from people aged 60 and over. Other potassium-raising medicines and heart failure are not counted, because no Thai use share or prevalence was found. The kidney count does include people who do not know they have kidney disease, so a warning that begins with a diagnosis reaches few of them (section 5.4).
6. **Claims.** On the per-serving and per-100 mL bases of Notification 445 the serve does not reach a potassium content claim (9.0% against 15%; 2.7% against 7.5%). The per-100 kilocalorie basis of Annex 4 Table 2 was not tested because no energy value exists for the serve. With the Notification's own conversion factors the cube cannot exceed 34.1 kcal, which would put it above the 10% line on that basis, so a claim may be technically open there. On the solid per 100 g basis of the same Table 2 (15% and 30% of the Thai RDI per 100 g), the cube as sold carries about 8,345 mg per 100 g, 238% of the Thai RDI, which is above both lines (7.9 times the 30% line), so as sold it would meet the "high in" line on that basis. Which basis applies to a dissolvable cube is not settled by the text, and conditions 1.1 and 2.2 of Annex 4 were not tested. The U.S. "good source" line (10% of the 4,700 mg daily value, 470 mg per reference amount) is not met on a 330 mL basis (6.7% of the daily value), but the U.S. reference amount was not read. "No potassium claim" is therefore the team's setting, because the claim is on the barred-claims list. It is not a consequence of the thresholds.
7. **Thai precedent for a potassium drink.** The Thai Department of Medical Sciences, as reported in a news article with no publication date of its own (S; the picture path in the page points to April 2021), gives three separate points. It limits coconut water to one fruit a day, and the limit follows the Department of Health sugar guideline of 32 g a day, so it is tied to sugar and blood sugar and not to potassium. It says people with degenerative kidney disease should avoid coconut water because it stimulates urination and dehydration may lead to heart failure, so that warning is tied to fluid and not to potassium. It names potassium only for people with an irregular heartbeat, for whom too much potassium may cause problems for the heart. The article is therefore a weak precedent for a potassium precaution on a drink, for the irregular heartbeat warning only. It is not support for a daily limit on the cube, which stays a plain assumption.
8. **Is 317 mg plausible for a general-population drink?** As a quantity it is ordinary: it equals about 0.38 of a 330 mL serve of coconut water and about 0.46 of a 330 mL serve of orange juice (USDA FoodData Central values, P; density from USDA's portion weights). As a lemon-lime drink it is unusual. A U.S. lemon-lime soda carries 1.00 mg of potassium per 100 g (USDA, P), about 3.4 mg per 330 mL, so the cube carries about 92 times that. The made-up drink is 24.5 mmol per litre, 1.23 times the WHO oral rehydration solution (S). Its form is a dissolved potassium salt with no food matrix, which is closer to the potassium effervescent products than to a food. The screen therefore supports a precaution and a medical view, and does not clear the serve.
9. **Settings and wording.** The team's proposed settings, the Gate 2 precaution wording in Thai and English, and the stated-limit text are in section 8. Nothing here has been reviewed by a clinician.

Three points for the reviewer. First, the cube is a potassium-containing agent of the kind UKKA Table 32 names, a dissolved potassium salt. The guideline does not mention drinks or cubes, so applying it to the cube is our reading. Second, KDIGO Practice Point 3.6.3 reads, as written (PDF page 44, P): "Hyperkalemia associated with use of RASi can often be managed by measures to reduce the serum potassium levels rather than decreasing the dose or stopping RASi." (RASi is KDIGO's term for ACE inhibitors and ARBs.) That describes managing high potassium by lowering it so that the drug can continue. KDIGO's own figures name the measures: Figure 21, hyperkalaemia branch, "Moderate dietary potassium intake", and Figure 32, first line, "Assess dietary potassium intake (dietary referral) and consider appropriate moderation of dietary potassium intake". So for people on these drugs the guideline's route to keeping the drug is to lower potassium, dietary potassium included; this supports a precaution. It gives no milligram figure. Third, none of the labels read, and none of the guidelines read, gives a threshold in milligrams below which a potassium salt is safe for these patients. They say monitor and consult. UKKA adds that risk is higher in renal impairment, diabetes mellitus, the elderly, use of more than one RAAS inhibitor and combinations of these drug groups (Table 9).

Which of these medicines are on the Thai National List of Essential Medicines was not checked (limit 11.2). The share of Thai adults taking each class other than ACE inhibitors and ARBs was not found (limit 11.5).

The difference that matters for a reviewer is form and delivery, not only size. The drinks and fruit above carry potassium inside a food matrix, absorbed at about 50% to 60% for plant foods (KDIGO). The cube delivers potassium as a dissolved salt in one drink, taken without food. KDIGO's 90% is its reported absorption of potassium salts added to processed foods (via Picard et al., 2021). Applying it to the dissolved cube is the team's extrapolation, since no absorption study of this product exists.

Thai precedent on coconut water (S; news report of a Department of Medical Sciences statement; `thai_dmsc_coconut_water_news_2026-10-20.md`). The article carries no publication date of its own: the only date on the page is the day-of-access banner. The picture path in the page body carries 2021/04/04 and the number 1617515402, which is 4 April 2021, 05:50 UTC, the upload time of the picture, so the article may date from April 2021; it is cited as undated. The article was re-read in this pass for the reasons it gives. ### 5.2 Method (DERIVED)

Each age band gets a low, mid and high value for each group. Overlaps are not double counted. For a union of groups, the low bound is the largest single group (full overlap), the high bound is the sum of the groups at their high inputs (no overlap, capped at 100%), and the mid value assumes the groups are independent. Independence at mid overstates the union if kidney disease and hypertension are positively correlated, which is expected but was not measured (no Thai joint distribution was read); the direction is that the true central value is at or below the mid value. Men's hypertension is the both-sex age table times the age-standardised men-to-all ratio (26.8 divided by 25.7); the high case multiplies young men's hypertension by 1.5 (ASSUMPTION). The young-adult treated share is an ASSUMPTION. Its low value is a PROXY estimate taken from the NHSO hospital sample: scaling the sample's age distribution (176 of 31,808 treated patients aged 25 to 34, 1,399 aged 35 to 44) to the model's national treated count gives 5.3% for ages 25 to 34 and 19.4% for 35 to 44. It is a proxy estimate from a public-hospital sample of patients already on treatment (mean age about 65). It is not a floor or lower bound for the national share, because nothing in the source makes it one. The mid value is the midpoint of that proxy and the all-age 47.9%, and the high value is 47.9%. For men aged 25 to 34 it sets the low and mid values (mid treated share 26.6%, which gives the 1.07% headline).

**What the range is.** The low value of the range is every input at its low value with full overlap, and the high value is every input at its high value with no overlap. The range is therefore a set of model bounds, not a statistical interval, and the central value is a midpoint of assumed bounds (the treated share, the ACE inhibitor and ARB share and the 60 and over kidney value are plain midpoints of their bounds). Section 5.3 shows which assumptions drive the width.

Kidney input. Low and mid values are the NHES V rows (CKD-EPI, 2014). The high value is the higher of the NHES V high row and the NHES III row (MDRD, 2004, both sexes) for the band, so every band's range spans both surveys. The NHES III row is the one that holds most of the band's ages: 2.3% for "25-34" (ages 26 to 29 sit in the 15 to 29 row at 0.4%, and ages 30 to 35 in the 30 to 44 row at 2.3%, so this is a ceiling for the youngest ages), 2.3% for "35-44", 13.1% for "45-59" and 39.8% for "60+" (ASSUMPTION for the mapping). For men the both-sex NHES III row is used unscaled, because the paper has no age table by sex; it therefore overstates men (their all-age NHES III share is 0.62 of the both-sex share). The Thai SEEK value (8.6%) has no age table and is used only as an all-age cross-check.

## 7. Is 317 mg a plausible serve for a general-population drink?

This is a screening judgement for a reviewer, not medical advice.

- **By quantity: ordinary.** The serve is within what ordinary foods and drinks carry: about 0.38 of a 330 mL serve of coconut water and about 0.46 of a 330 mL serve of orange juice (USDA, P). It is 9.0% of the Thai reference intake.
- **By drink type: unusual.** A U.S. lemon-lime soda carries 1.00 mg of potassium per 100 g (USDA, P), so a 330 mL can carries about 3.4 mg. The cube drink carries about 92 times that. Among sports drinks, the one label read carries 70 mg per packet (P for that product). The made-up drink is 1.23 times the potassium concentration of the WHO oral rehydration solution (S).
- **By form: a concentrated salt, not a food.** The potassium is a dissolved salt in one drink, taken without food. KDIGO reports about 90% absorption for potassium salts in processed foods. Applying that to the cube is the team's extrapolation.
- **Against potassium products regulated as drugs: below, but not far below.** The serve is 0.81 of one Effer-K 10 mEq tablet (391 mg), 0.68 of one Sando-K tablet (derived), and 0.32 of Klor-Con/EF (978 mg). It is 3.17 times the 100 mg line of 21 CFR 201.306 and 3.2 times the 99 mg over-the-counter convention. Those drug products treat low potassium and carry hyperkalaemia contraindications.
- **Against the Thai coconut statement.** On the USDA value coconut water carries about 2.5 mg of potassium per mL, against about 0.96 mg per mL for the cube drink, so a 330 mL serve of coconut water carries about 2.6 times the cube's potassium (837 mg against 316.6 mg; DERIVED). The Department of Medical Sciences statement (S) warns people with kidney disease off coconut water for fluid reasons (urination and dehydration), limits it to one fruit a day because of sugar, and names potassium only for people with an irregular heartbeat. It is a weak precedent for that one caution and sets no limit for potassium. The Thai potassium figure for coconut water was not separated from chloride, so the quantity comparison rests on the USDA value.
- **By people: small in the lead segment, larger elsewhere.** The groups in section 5 are small in the lead segment (about 1% narrow, up to 5.5% at the model's upper bound), larger in the volume segment (about 8% narrow, up to 20.5%), and largest in older adults. Kidney disease is mostly unknown to the people who have it (1.9% aware of any stage in Thai SEEK).

What settles it, and what a desk screen cannot do: a physician or clinical pharmacist view on whether one serve of potassium salt is acceptable for a population with no labelled warning, whether a daily limit is needed, and whether a Thai regulator classes a potassium bicarbonate cube as a food or as a potassium product. Those are Gate 2 items. This screen finds the serve plausible as a food quantity and not trivial as a potassium salt in a soft drink. It supports a precaution and a medical view, not clearance.
