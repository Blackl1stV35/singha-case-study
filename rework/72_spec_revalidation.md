## 2. CO2: 0.55 volume per 330 mL

Basis. A volume of CO2 is the gas volume at standard conditions per volume of liquid. The molar volume is V = R T / P with R = 8.314462618 J/(mol K) (CODATA 2022, P, exact) and P = 101,325 Pa. At 0 C that gives 22.414 L/mol, which is the basis of the slide's 0.55 (DERIVED from P). At the IUPAC standard pressure of 1 bar and 0 C the molar volume is 22.711 L/mol; the slide's basis is 1 atm. A second convention in use counts the volume at 60 F (15.56 C; US Patent 4,636,337, S); on it the same dose reads 0.58 volume. The slide basis is 0 C.

| Reference temperature | Molar volume, L/mol (1 atm) | CO2 from the 0.55 dose, mL | Volumes per 330 mL | KHCO3 for 0.55 volume, g | Potassium, mg |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0 C | 22.414 | 181.5 | 0.550 | 0.8107 | 316.6 |
| 60 F (15.56 C) | 23.690 | 191.8 | 0.581 | 0.7670 | 299.5 |
| 20 C | 24.055 | 194.8 | 0.590 | 0.7554 | 295.0 |
| 30 C | 24.876 | 201.4 | 0.610 | 0.7305 | 285.3 |

The 0.81 g used on the slide releases 181.3 mL at 0 C, which rounds to the same 0.55.

Dissolved capacity. The Wikipedia data page (S) gives 0.878 volume of CO2 at 20 C and 101.3 kPa per volume of water, normalised to 0 C. Pure CO2 at 1 atm could therefore hold more than the 0.55 to 0.61 volumes the reaction makes, so a sealed pack would hold the gas dissolved at modest pressure. How much stays in an open glass is not measured. The slide must read "gas released at 0 C and 1 atmosphere", not "carbonation".

## 4. Citric acid and pH

Stoichiometry. 3 KHCO3 + C6H8O7 gives K3C6H5O7 + 3 CO2 + 3 H2O. One mole of citric acid neutralises three moles of bicarbonate, so 0.81 g needs 0.518 g of anhydrous citric acid (0.519 g at the 0.8107 g dose). The 2.10 g is 4.05 times that amount. Molar masses: KHCO3 100.114 g/mol and citric acid 192.123 g/mol (IUPAC 2023, P).

Model. Open vessel at 25 C. Potassium (all of it) plus H+ equals the citrate and carbonate anion charge plus OH-. CO2 leaves as it forms, so the carbonate left is the bicarbonate not neutralised. pKa values for citric acid are 3.128, 4.761 and 6.396 at zero ionic strength (S, Wikipedia). Carbonate pK2 is 10.33 (S), and pK1 is 6.35 (ASSUMPTION, textbook).

Two activity readings, stated exactly. The Davies model gives apparent dissociation constants, and the charge balance is solved for the H+ concentration. The solved value is the "concentration pH", the negative log of the H+ concentration. A pH meter reads the H+ activity, which is lower than the concentration by the factor gamma, so the meter-equivalent pH is the concentration pH plus A f (0.0673 at 2.10 g, ionic strength 0.027 M). The first pass reported the concentration pH as if it were what a meter reads, and gave 1.85 g for a meter pH of 3.5. The corrected values are below.

The pH at the potassium citrate point is 9.15, but it is very sharp: at exactly 0.52 g there is 0.27 percent excess acid over the stoichiometric 0.5186 g and the ideal pH has already fallen to 8.46. The slide's 0.52 g is a rounded stoichiometric amount, not a pH target. With no acid at all the bicarbonate alone gives pH 8.34 (ideal).

Mass for a target pH, in g (ideal / Davies concentration pH / Davies meter pH): pH 3.0, 3.693 / 3.070 / 3.364; pH 3.3, 2.535 / 2.191 / 2.341; pH 3.5, 2.101 / 1.848 / 1.949; pH 3.7, 1.810 / 1.604 / 1.679; pH 4.0, 1.515 / 1.337 / 1.392; pH 4.5, 1.175 / 1.022 / 1.060.

Sensitivity to pKa at 2.10 g (ideal model). pKa1 plus or minus 0.1 moves the pH to 3.58 or 3.42. pKa2 plus or minus 0.1 gives 3.52 or 3.48. pKa3 has no effect at this pH.

Reading. The ideal model reproduces 3.50 at 2.10 g, so the slide's 3.5 matches that model. If the working pH is read on a meter, the Davies sensitivity says it could read about 3.4 at 2.10 g, and 1.95 g would be needed for 3.5 on the meter. The ideal value stays the working value because the pKa values are secondary and the Davies correction is an approximation. The meter reading is a stated limit. Slide 7 should say "working pH 3.5 by charge-balance model". The team's separate chemistry record (rework/71, T8) solves the same balance at 20 C with temperature-dependent constants and reads 3.42 as the activity pH, 3.498 on the ideal basis at 25 C, which agrees with the values here.

Acid load. 2.10 g in 0.330 L is 6.36 g of citric acid per litre (0.64 percent w/v). The sourness comes from this load, not from the pH. Section 5 compares it with published and starting-formula levels.

## 5. Sucralose, and the sweet and sour balance

- Share of the cube. 33 mg is 0.87 percent of the 3.79 g dry mass (a blending item for pressing).
- Sweetness. Sucralose is about 600 times sweeter than table sugar (FDA, P, content current 27 February 2025). 33 mg is therefore 6.0 g of sugar equivalent per 100 mL. This is a derived anchor, not a sensory test.
- Regulation (P). Notification 468 (in force from 12 December 2025) sets sucralose at 300 mg/kg for category 14.1.4 flavoured drinks, with conditions 127 and TH3. The row and both conditions were first read on 19 October 2026 and reread on 20 October 2026 from the local copy of the Annex as page images (PDF pages 678, 789 and 836; saved in `thai_notification_468_rows_reconfirmed_2026-10-20.md`); the file hash matches the one recorded on 19 October. - Intake. EU ADI 15 mg/kg bw per day (EFSA plain-language summary, 16 February 2026, P): one serve is 3.7 percent of the ADI for a 60 kg adult and 14.7 percent for a 15 kg child. FDA ADI 5 mg/kg bw per day (P): one serve is 11 percent for an adult and 44 percent for a 15 kg child.
- Sweet and sour benchmarks (both pages opened again on 20 October 2026 and saved in `taste_and_acid_benchmarks.md`).

(P): 2.50 percent w/v sucrose alone had a mean sweetness of 6.32 on a 9-point scale, and with 0.140 percent w/v citric acid 4.53, a drop of 28.3 percent (our arithmetic), reported as suppression of sweetness by the acids. The design's sugar equivalent (6.0 g per 100 mL) is 2.4 times the 2.50 percent sucrose, so the acid per unit of sweetness is about 1.9 times the Foods 2020 sample (0.106 against 0.056 g acid per g sugar equivalent).
  - Reading. The acid load is about 2.9 times an ingredient maker's lemon-lime starting formula, and 33 mg of sucralose may be low against it. This is an untasted desk flag, not a finding: the Foods 2020 panel used sucrose in water, not sucralose, and nothing made from the cube has been tasted. The first lever at the first tasting is more sweetener or less acid, and less acid moves the pH up (section 4).

## 7. The 14 mm cube and compaction plausibility

- Volume 14 x 14 x 14 mm = 2.744 cm3. 3.79 g gives a density of 1.381 g/cm3 (DERIVED). The slide's "14 mm at 3.79 g" is internally consistent.
- True density of the mix (volume-additive): 1.695 g/cm3, using KHCO3 2.17 and citric acid 1.665 g/cm3 (S, Wikipedia), and binder 1.50, flavour 1.00 and sucralose 1.69 g/cm3 (ASSUMPTIONS). Porosity is therefore about 18.5 percent, and 13.6 to 19.7 percent if the binder density is 1.2 to 1.6 g/cm3 (DERIVED, relative density 0.815).
- Across the allowance range the same 14 mm cube would be 1.262 g/cm3 and 26.8 percent porous at 3.46 g, and 1.547 g/cm3 and 6.9 percent porous at 4.24 g (same true-density assumptions; script key cube.range_ends_14mm_cube). So 14 mm holds as designed near the central mass. At the top of the range the porosity is about half the 13.7 percent a tablet reached at 159 MPa (below), which would call for more compaction than any sourced tablet, and at the bottom of the range the cube is a loose body. The range is a formulation allowance, and it does not mean cubes of both masses are pressed in the same die.
- Benchmarks read and saved in `effervescent_tablet_benchmarks.md` and `effervescent_hardness_benchmarks_N.md`:
  - Pharmaceutics 2025;17(5):676 (P, opened again): 11.28 mm flat punches, 20 kN stated as 200 MPa (our check: 200.1 MPa), target mass about 350 mg, calculated true density 1.8246 g/cm3, tensile strength above the 2 MPa acceptance level, disintegration 62.7 to 77.8 s. No porosity or breaking force is in the text.
  - RSC Advances 2019 (P, from the saved extraction, NOT reconfirmed: the page returned a browser check on the second pass): 8.5 mm flat punches at 53, 106 and 159 MPa; porosity of uncoated effervescent granule tablets 13.67 percent at 159 MPa (relative density 0.863).
  - Jungbunzlauer facts sheet (S, read as page images in the third pass): 5 g tablets of 25 mm at 70 kN (142.6 MPa by our arithmetic), 2 percent PEG 4000, hardness 67.9 N for a hand mix, 117.8 N for the supplier's compound at 70 kN and 70.2 N at 44 kN (n = 20, axis labelled N).
  - Hardness in newtons from four sources (new in the third pass): 40.53 N (Ansar et al. 2009, fruit juice effervescent tablet, P, abstract only), 72.3 +/- 5.5 N (Taymouri et al. 2019, bismuth subcitrate, P, abstract only), 67.9 to 117.8 N (Jungbunzlauer, S) and 100 N (Roquette vitamin C tablet, 2,400 mg, 20 mm flat punch, S). So reported effervescent tablet hardness runs from about 40 to 118 N. These are breaking forces of round flat tablets. A 14 mm cube has no diameter and no sourced hardness benchmark exists for a cube, so the cube's hardness is not predicted: stated limit. The amoxicillin tablet paper prints no hardness unit and is not used.
  - Density. No sourced bulk density of a commercial effervescent tablet could be derived, because none of the opened sources gives tablet thickness. The one figure found (1.250 g/cm3, a 2023 conference abstract) and the patent hardness and porosity ranges could not be opened (HTTP 403 and 404) and stay UNVERIFIED. The brief's "typical effervescent tablet density" is therefore closed as a stated limit; the design density is compared with the 13.7 percent porosity the RSC tablet reached (below).
- Pressing force (DERIVED). A cube pressed in a square die has a 14 x 14 mm = 196 mm2 face. At the pressures the sources used, 142.6, 159 and 200 MPa, the force is 28.0, 31.2 and 39.2 kN. No source gives a press capacity or a force for a cube.
- Mass per unit of punch face (DERIVED, a proxy for the depth of the powder fill). The cube carries 19.34 mg per mm2 of face, against 3.50 (Pharmaceutics 2025), 7.64 (Roquette) and 10.19 (Jungbunzlauer), so 1.90 to 5.52 times the sourced tablets: a deeper fill, with a larger pressure gradient from the punch to the die floor.
- Density against the benchmark. The design porosity (18.5 percent) is not tighter than the 13.7 percent the RSC tablet reached at 159 MPa, so the central design density does not call for more compaction than a tablet reached. The composition and the granule type differ, so this is a plausibility check only.
- Shape limit. The sourced tablets are round, flat and thin. The Pharmaceutics tablet is about 1.9 to 2.4 mm thick (350 mg at the paper's true density with 0 to 20 percent porosity), a height to diameter ratio of 0.17 to 0.21. The design cube has a height equal to its width (ratio 1.0) and about 11 times the mass of that tablet. A cube is not a tablet shape: density gradients, die-wall friction, ejection, lamination, hardness and dissolution time of a cube-shaped body are not measured, and no source for a cube was found. These are stated limits.
- The cube side at a density of 1.60 to 1.25 g/cm3 for 3.79 g is 13.3 to 14.5 mm (DERIVED), which brackets the design.

## 8. Chemistry and regulation flags

1. Sweet and sour balance (main taste flag). 33 mg of sucralose matches 6.0 g of sugar per 100 mL, and the added citric acid is 6.4 g per litre, about 2.9 times a lemon-lime starting formula and 4.5 times the level that suppressed sweetness by 28 percent in a published sucrose panel (section 5). This is not a regulatory conflict, since the Thai limit is 300 mg/kg. It is an untasted balance and the first lever at the first tasting.
2. pH against taste. The pH is not what makes the drink sour. The cube drink at pH 3.5 is above three measured soft drinks: Sprite 2.71, Coca-Cola 2.56 and Schweppes Bitter Lemon 2.54 (Barac et al. 2023, Acta Medica Medianae 62(3), Table 2, P, one Serbian bottling each, read on opening; saved by T8 in `T8/barac2023_sprite_pH_TA.md`). On the ideal value that is 0.79, 0.94 and 0.96 pH units higher, and 6.2, 8.7 and 9.1 times less hydrogen ion (5.1, 7.1 and 7.5 times on the meter-equivalent value). The sourness comes from the acid load. From the team's chemistry record (rework/71, T8's calculation, not rerun here): the titratable acidity of the degassed cube to pH 7.0 is 1.33 times that of Sprite (1.72 with all CO2 retained), and the citric acid mass is about 5.3 times a Sprite sample (6.36 against 1.20 g/L; S, Ali et al. 2022 abstract). So the acid load, not the pH, is the taste issue, and it is already flag 1.
3. Gas basis. 0.55 volume is gas released at 0 C and 1 atm, not retained gas. The slide must say so.
4. 14 mm at 3.79 g is consistent with a density of 1.38 g/cm3. At the top of the range (4.24 g) the density rises to 1.55 g/cm3 and the estimated porosity falls to 6.9 percent for the same 14 mm side (section 7), which is tighter than the benchmark tablet. Section 7 gives the pressing limits.
5. Net weight. 3.79 g is the central mass and 3.8 g is the label rounding, to be confirmed by weighing before print. Notification 195 B.E. 2543 (Thai FDA compilation copy, page 1 read as an image, P, our translation; `thai_notification_195_mineral_salt_beverage.md`) defines a mineral-salt beverage as a drink in which mineral salts are the main ingredient, and requires per litre sodium 460 to 920 mg, sugar of at least 2 percent (glucose or fructose) or 4 percent (sucrose), potassium at most 195 mg, bicarbonate at most 793 mg and citrate at most 819 mg. The cube drink has no sodium by design, no sugar, about 959 mg of potassium per litre (4.9 times the ceiling) and about 6.3 to 6.4 g of citrate per litre (7.6 to 7.8 times the ceiling). It therefore could not be sold as a mineral-salt beverage. The sucralose row of Notification 468 carries conditions 127 and TH3. The row and both condition texts were reread on 20 October 2026 from the local copy of the Annex as page images (the fetch tool cannot open the 16 MB file; the local file hash matches the one recorded on 19 October). Condition TH3 reads, in our translation: for mineral-salt beverage products, the additive must be used together with sugar of the type and amount set in the mineral-salt beverage notification (Annex printed page 835). So TH3 ties sucralose use in a mineral-salt beverage to sugar and does not bind a flavoured drink. The two saved readings of TH3 therefore tie it to Notification 195 but differ in the direction of the condition: the saved 19 October extract of Notification 468 reads it as a sugar-pairing duty for electrolyte drinks, which the 20 October page confirms, and the saved page of the repealed Notification 418 read it as an exclusion of Notification 195 products, which is superseded. The cube drink has no sugar and fails the Notification 195 composition, so it could not be sold as a mineral-salt beverage in any case. The setting the team proposes is the flavoured-drink class (category 14.1.4). Its basis is that the pack makes no electrolyte, mineral or hydration claim, that the drink fails the mineral-salt beverage composition, and that citric acid, not a mineral salt, is the largest solid (55 percent of the cube dry mass; after the reaction about 48 percent of the dissolved solids are citric acid and 25 percent potassium citrate; DERIVED). Whether the Thai FDA agrees is not settled by the text read, so the classification is a stated limit.

Page 2 of the explanatory announcement of Notification 468 (P, Thai text, our translation; `thai_notification_468_explanatory_announcement_quantum_satis.md`) defines quantum satis as the lowest amount of the additive that gives the desired technological effect under good manufacturing practice, says it does not mean the additive may be added without limit, and says the operator must study its own product to find that lowest amount.
